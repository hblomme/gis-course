10.3 Transformation types

Geometric deviations

When geographic coordinates are calculated automatically from control points, a complication often arises: the relative positions of the control points on the image do not perfectly match those of the given coordinates (or homologous points). This can happen, for example, because of cartographic errors or because the control points were not defined precisely.

The map below (left), made by Pieter van der Beke in 1538, illustrates the problem. It shows the area around Antwerp and the Waasland region (in the north of present-day Belgium). Note that north is not at the top of this map. That is why the map is strongly rotated after georeferencing in the images further down.

When you connect Boechout (B), Tielrode (T), Nieuwkerken-Waas (N) and Antwerp (A) on this map with straight lines, the four places lie roughly on the corners of a rectangle. On a present-day map (right), however, the same four places are far from forming a rectangle. This means that Pieter van der Beke’s map cannot simply be overlaid on a present-day map without geometric distortion.

Pieter van der Beke, De Charte van Vlaendren, 1538 (detail). Bibliothèque nationale de France, Gallica, via Wikimedia Commons, public domain. Background map right © OpenStreetMap contributors.

Residuals

The map can still be georeferenced, but you will have to accept that the control points do not all match their corresponding geographic coordinates exactly. The software positions the map so that the control points lie as close as possible to these coordinates. In practice, it calculates a transformation that minimises the overall residual error. The orange lines in the image below show the deviation of each control point. This deviation is called the residual.

The map is moved and rotated, but not distorted: the control points (blue) do not exactly match the given coordinates (red). Background map © OpenStreetMap contributors.

Deformations

Alternatively, you can distort the image (by stretching, bending, etc.) so that the control points exactly match their corresponding geographic coordinates. This can involve substantial deformation of the original map, or ‘warping’.

The map is distorted (‘rubber-sheeting’), so that the control points match the given coordinates exactly. Background map © OpenStreetMap contributors.

Various intermediate solutions between the options above are also possible, in which the image you are georeferencing is distorted to a certain extent and the positions of the control points deviate to a certain extent from the given coordinates.

Below we discuss the different options that QGIS offers for this.

Seven transformation types

As you saw above, the map you are georeferencing rarely fits the base map perfectly. QGIS therefore offers different transformation types, each allowing different kinds of geometric adjustments to fit the map to its geographic reference. Each transformation type uses a different mathematical approach to establish the relationship between the map’s pixel coordinates and real-world coordinates.

Some transformations allow only limited geometric changes, while others offer greater flexibility to accommodate discrepancies between the original map and the control points. Understanding these differences is essential for choosing an appropriate transformation type. As a general rule, the greater the allowed distortions, the more freedom the transformation has to fit the GCPs, and the smaller the residuals are likely to be.

QGIS offers seven transformation types. The table below compares their main properties, the minimum number of ground control points (GCPs) required, and situations in which each transformation type may be appropriate. The visual examples illustrate the kinds of geometric changes each transformation can produce. Click an example to enlarge it.

Although the minimum number of control points required by QGIS may be relatively low, in practice you need many control points, well distributed across the map.

Which type should you choose?

Knowing the properties of the different transformation types is only the first step. The more important question is which one to use for a particular map.

There is no universally best transformation type. The appropriate choice depends on the nature of the geometric discrepancies, the quality of the control points and the purpose of the georeferencing.

If you know the coordinate reference system (CRS) in which the map was originally created, it is good practice to use that same CRS while georeferencing (see Running the georeferencing). This avoids introducing additional discrepancies simply because of a difference in map projection, and may reduce the need for more complex transformations.

If the original CRS is unknown, or if the map does not correspond to a standard projection, you will need to assess the geometric discrepancies in the map itself.

The following considerations can help you make an informed choice.

Inconsistencies in the control points

If your control points show clear residuals with a transformation type that distorts little (such as Helmert or Polynomial 1), consider their cause: are they mainly due to geometric errors in the original map, or to inaccuracies in how you placed the control points?

  • If the original map contains geometric errors, a more flexible transformation may be appropriate, as it can compensate for these distortions.
  • If the discrepancies are mainly caused by imprecise placement of control points, a less flexible transformation is usually preferable. This helps prevent small inaccuracies in individual control points from distorting the map.

QGIS calculates a transformation that minimises the overall residuals. A more flexible transformation can therefore produce smaller residuals without necessarily producing a more accurate result. The residuals only reflect the average deviation between the coordinates you assigned to the control points and the positions where these points end up after georeferencing. They say nothing about the accuracy of the rest of the map.

Inaccuracies of the map

If you conclude that the residuals are mainly caused by geometric errors in the original map, the next step is to examine the nature of these errors.

  • Systematic (global) errors follow the same pattern across the whole map. Consider the nature and size of the deformation:
    • Is curvature needed? Then consider Polynomial 2, or Polynomial 3 for more complex curvature.
    • Is there a perspective distortion, as in aerial photographs and oblique images? Then consider Projective.
  • For local, irregular errors, which are common in historical maps, consider Thin plate spline. Alternatively, you can accept larger residuals and choose a transformation type that introduces less distortion.

Aim of the georeferencing

What is the aim of the georeferencing?

  • Preserving the original appearance of the map: If the main purpose is to preserve the map’s original appearance, for example for publication or visual analysis, prefer a transformation that introduces as little geometric distortion as possible. A transformation that slightly compromises the fit of individual control points may be preferable to one that substantially changes the shape of the original map.
  • Achieving geometric accuracy: If the main purpose is to align the map as closely as possible with its geographic reference, you may need a more flexible transformation. Choose the transformation type that best addresses the geometric inaccuracies in the map, while taking care not to introduce implausible deformations.

Whatever your aim, always check the map after georeferencing to assess the result.
