8.5 Rule-based styling

Rules

In chapter 4 you learned that labels and diagrams can be added to vector features, while the appearance of geometries can be controlled through their colour, shape and other visual properties. These styles are often linked to attribute values.

However, some situations require more flexibility. A map may need labels for only the largest cities, or different colours for features that meet a combination of conditions.

Rule-based styling makes this possible: it allows you to create rules in which you define a condition and link it to a specific style. QGIS then applies that style to all features that meet the condition.

The following examples illustrate how rules work for both labels and symbology.

Rule-based labels: example

Consider a point layer containing the major cities of the United States. The aim is to label only the most populated cities, with the label size reflecting their population. Each line below has a condition before the colon and a style after it:

  • more than 2 000 000 inhabitants: font size 20 pt, black
  • 1 000 000 to 2 000 000 inhabitants: font size 14 pt, dark grey
  • 500 000 to 1 000 000 inhabitants: font size 9 pt, grey
  • fewer than 500 000 inhabitants: no label

This requires three rules, one for each group of cities that should receive a label. Cities outside these groups will remain unlabelled.

Step 1: Open the rule-based labeling settings

In the layer’s Layer Properties, open the Labels tab and select Rule-based Labeling at the top (A1).

The large empty area (A2) is the rule overview. It is initially empty because no rules have been defined.

Step 2: Define the first rule

Click the green plus symbol at the bottom of the window (A3) to create the first rule. This opens the Edit Rule window.

Start by entering a name or description in the Description field (B1). The remaining settings are organised into two main sections:

  • the condition that identifies the relevant features (B2)
  • the label settings that determine how these features appear on the map (B3)

The condition goes in the Filter field and takes the form of an expression. Expressions can be typed directly into this field or entered through the full expression builder, opened with the button (B4). Make sure the result of your expression is either TRUE or FALSE, allowing QGIS to check whether each feature meets the condition or not. Typical examples include comparisons such as =, < and >, as well as functions such as intersects().

In our case we enter "POPULATION" > 2000000. This condition gives TRUE for the cities with more than two million inhabitants.

Next we define how the labels of these cities should look (B3). This section works in the same way as Single Labels. Select the field NAME as the label value and set the font size to 20 pt and the colour to black.

Click OK to save the rule.

Step 3: Repeat the previous step for every rule you need

After you have created this first rule, you can create the second and third ones in the same way. As the condition (in the Filter field), enter "POPULATION" > 1000000 AND "POPULATION" <= 2000000 for the second rule and "POPULATION" > 500000 AND "POPULATION" <= 1000000 for the third. (With <= cities with exactly 2 000 000 or 1 000 000 inhabitants are also included.) For the style, you again label the features based on the field NAME, but this time with a font size of 14 pt (dark grey) and 9 pt (grey). Click OK to add each rule.

All the rules you created are now shown in the rule overview (A2). To edit a rule, double-click it. You can delete a rule with the red minus symbol (A3).

When you have created the rules and click OK at the bottom of the Layer Properties, you immediately see the desired result in your map canvas: the largest labels for the 4 cities above 2 000 000 inhabitants, medium-sized labels for the 6 cities between 1 000 000 and 2 000 000, small labels for the 26 cities between 500 000 and 1 000 000, and no labels for the other cities.

Rule-based symbology: example

Rules are equally useful for controlling the symbology of geometries. The next example demonstrates how several conditions can be combined to distinguish different groups of features.

Consider a polygon layer containing the US states. The objective is to classify the states according to two criteria:

  • Area: is the state larger than 300 000 km²?
  • Population: did the state have more than 10 million inhabitants in 2010?

These two criteria produce four possible groups:

  • states larger than 300 000 km² with more than 10 million inhabitants
  • states larger than 300 000 km² with 10 million inhabitants or fewer
  • states of 300 000 km² or less with more than 10 million inhabitants
  • states of 300 000 km² or less with 10 million inhabitants or fewer

Each group requires a different colour.

A classification based on a single attribute, such as Categorized symbology, is not well suited to this task. Rule-based symbology offers a more flexible solution because each rule can combine multiple conditions.

Step 1: Open the rule-based symbology settings

In the layer’s Layer Properties, open the Symbology tab and select Rule-based at the top (C1).

Step 2: Define the required rules

As in the previous example, you add the rules one by one with the green plus symbol. The rule overview (C2) shows the four rules of this example, each with its filter expression and its colour.

The Else rule

A feature that matches none of the rules gets no label and no symbol. That is why the fourth group of cities in the first example needs no rule of its own. If you do want to give all remaining features a style, create a rule with Else instead of a filter (Catch-all for other features).

Features that meet several rules

A feature that matches several rules gets the style of each of them. In that case QGIS draws the symbols of all matching rules on top of each other, in the order of the rule list. This means that the last rule in the list lies on top. If a style covers the styles below it, those are of course no longer visible.

The map of the United States above shows the combined result of the two examples.
